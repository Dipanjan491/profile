01 — Philosophy

The mind, like weather, arrives — and, given room, passes.

Most distress isn't a malfunction to be fixed. It's a signal — sometimes loud, sometimes old — asking to be understood. My work begins here: not with urgency, but with attention. And not with labels, but with careful measurement that illuminates rather than reduces.

As both a clinician and a psychometrician, I inhabit two worlds that are too often separated. In one, the human being in front of me is the primary data. In the other, it's the rigorous statistical models — Item Response Theory, latent profile analysis, construct validation — that help us see structure in what otherwise feels like noise.

Good measurement and good therapy share the same posture: curiosity without presumption. Whether I'm constructing a scale, validating an assessment, or sitting with someone across the consulting room, the question is always — what is this trying to tell us?

07 — Approach

Five commitments that shape the work.

i.

Measurement before interpretation.

A score is not a verdict. Before we name what someone is experiencing, we look at what the instruments actually tell us — and what they don't. IRT, CTT, and latent profile analysis aren't just statistical techniques; they're disciplines of humility. They tell us what our tools can and cannot see.

ii.

Slowness as method.

Quick fixes are rarely durable ones. I work at the pace the work requires — sometimes that means six sessions, sometimes sixty. We will not rush toward insight before the ground has been prepared for it.

iii.

Evidence without dogma.

Good research matters. So does the irreducible particularity of one person's life. I draw on the literature — and I remain skeptical of any framework that asks us to ignore what is sitting in front of us.

iv.

Scale construction as care.

Building a psychological instrument is an act of ethical attention. Every item, every response option, every validated factor structure is a decision about what we consider worth measuring — and who we consider worth measuring it for. The Indian context demands Indian instruments, not borrowed ones.

v.

Teaching as transmission.

Supervision and teaching are not side work — they are how the field continues. I take seriously the responsibility of training the next generation of psychologists to be both computationally literate and clinically present. R workshops, M.Phil supervision, curriculum design: all the same practice, in different registers.
